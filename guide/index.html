Candy Merge 2048 guide: the chain, the scoring and how to reach the Watermelon

Updated October 28, 2026 · by KOGAMES · Play Candy Merge 2048 · Update notes

If you have played any "suika"-style merge game you already know the loop: drop a candy, two matching candies touch, they merge into the next one up, and the jar slowly fills. What decides whether you make the Watermelon or top out at a sad Coconut is mostly jar management. This guide lays out the exact chain, what every merge is worth, how the jar decides a game is over, and the handful of habits that separate a 3,000-point run from a 10,000-point one. All numbers come from the game's own balance file.

The eleven candies

Every candy merges only with its twin and becomes the next one in the line. From the smallest to the Watermelon:

1. Blueberrydropped only
2. Cherry+6 pts
3. Grape+12 pts
4. Lemon+20 pts
5. Orange+30 pts
6. Apple+42 pts
7. Peach+56 pts
8. Coconut+72 pts
9. Pineapple+90 pts
10. Melon+110 pts
11. Watermelon+132 pts

Two Watermelons touching is the only merge that does not create a bigger candy: both vanish, you score a big bonus and get 10 gems, and the jar suddenly has a lot of room again. Making a second Watermelon in the same game is the real "win" of Candy Merge.

How scoring works

You score when candies merge, never when you drop. A merge is worth twice the merge value of the candy it creates: a Cherry is 6 points, a Lemon 20, a Coconut 72, a Watermelon 132. Two Watermelons clearing each other pay 332 points.

Because every big candy is built from many small merges, the points add up along the way. Building one Watermelon from nothing (1,024 Blueberries' worth of candy) earns about 2,900 points in merges before the Watermelon itself. That is why a score under 1,000 usually means the jar filled up before anything large existed, and why scores climb quickly once you keep a jar alive long enough to hold two or three big candies at once.

The jar and the dead line

The jar is 7.2 units wide and 9 deep; a Watermelon is 3.4 units across, so two of them already take up most of the width. Near the top there is a line. A candy that has settled above that line for 1.2 seconds ends the game. Candies that are still falling, bouncing or sliding do not count, and nothing counts during the 3 seconds after a continue.

In practice that means you lose when the pile is tall and still. A candy that pokes over the line while things are still moving gives you a moment to react — a merge lower down, or a Shake, can pull it back below the line before the timer runs out.

Five habits that win games

  1. Keep big candies on one side, small on the other. The classic layout is a slope: Watermelon and Melon against the left wall, then descending sizes towards the right. Small drops land on the small side, merge upwards, and roll downhill into the big ones.
  2. Never bury a small candy. A Blueberry trapped under a Pineapple is dead weight that can only be freed by a merge above it. If a small candy is about to be covered, spend a drop clearing it first.
  3. Drop onto the match, not next to it. Physics in this game are soft: a candy dropped directly on top of its twin merges before it can roll away. Dropped beside it, it often slips into a gap instead.
  4. Use the walls. A candy dropped at the very edge rolls inward along the slope. Use that to nudge a mid-size candy into its twin without disturbing the pile.
  5. Merge big before small when you can choose. Clearing two Coconuts frees far more room than two Grapes, and room is what keeps the game alive.

The one-move look-ahead. Before every drop, ask two questions: "what does this merge now?" and "what does it block later?" Most lost games come from a drop that merged nicely but sealed a small candy underneath it.

Shake, Remove and Continue

  • Shake (30 gems, first one free each game) jolts the whole jar. Use it when a candy is sitting over the dead line with a gap it could slide into, or when two twins are a hair apart. It does nothing useful on a tightly packed pile.
  • Remove (20 gems, up to 3 per game) deletes one candy you tap. The best target is a small candy buried deep on the "big" side — removing it lets the heavy candies settle and often triggers a merge for free.
  • Continue when the game ends: the first one is free, then 100, 200, 300, 500 and 800 gems. You pick a spot and every candy in a circle around it is cleared, and you get 3 seconds of safety. Choosing the top of the tallest column is the obvious move; choosing a cluster of small candies on top of a big one is often better, because it leaves the big candy exposed for a merge.

Controls and auto-drop

On a phone, drag to aim and lift your finger to drop. On a keyboard, ← → or A D move the candy and Space, Enter or ↓ drop it. AUTO drops wherever you are aiming every 1.4 seconds; it is handy for farming the early chain but turn it off once the pile gets tall, because every drop then needs a decision.

Quick answers

  • Why didn't two identical candies merge? They have to touch while at least one of them is free to move. If both are wedged, nudge the pile with a nearby drop or a Shake.
  • Does the Watermelon ever drop from the top? No. Only the first five candies are dealt; everything larger must be built.
  • Is my score shared? Only if you enter a nickname for the world ranking; the ranking stores your best score and nickname, nothing else.
  • What is a good score? A first Watermelon usually lands you around 3,000–4,000. Keeping two Watermelons' worth of candy alive and clearing them pushes past 8,000.
